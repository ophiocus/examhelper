# Primary Sources

The study of Gnosticism draws on four overlapping bodies of evidence. Each has its own limits, and the picture only becomes coherent when they are read against one another.

## 1. The heresiological tradition

Before the modern manuscript discoveries, almost everything known about the gnostics came from the Church Fathers who wrote against them.

- **Irenaeus of Lyon**, *Against Heresies* (c. 180). The earliest and most detailed systematic refutation; Book I summarizes the Valentinians and the Sethian-Ophite mythology. Irenaeus is the source for the classical account of Sophia's fall.
- **Hippolytus of Rome**, *Refutation of All Heresies* (c. 220). Preserves longer quotations, including the Naassene hymn and the *Apophasis Megale* attributed to Simon Magus.
- **Tertullian**, *Against the Valentinians*, *Against Marcion*, *Against Hermogenes* (early 3rd c.). Polemical but rhetorically vivid.
- **Clement of Alexandria**, *Stromata* and *Excerpta ex Theodoto* (c. 200). Sympathetic in tone; preserves authentic Valentinian fragments.
- **Origen**, *Against Celsus* and *Commentary on John* (mid 3rd c.). Engages Heracleon's Valentinian commentary on John in detail.
- **Epiphanius of Salamis**, *Panarion* ("Medicine Chest", c. 375). A late encyclopedic catalogue of eighty heresies; valuable but often garbled.

These authors give us names, schools, and the outline of mythologies. They rarely quote at length, and when they do, they select passages meant to scandalize.

## 2. The pre-Nag Hammadi Coptic codices

Three codices reached European collections in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries:

- **Askew Codex** (British Library MS Add. 5114). Acquired by Dr. Anthony Askew in 1772. Contains the *Pistis Sophia* — four books of dialogue between the risen Christ and his disciples. The standard critical edition is Carl Schmidt's (1905), with English translations by G. R. S. Mead (1896, rev. 1921) and Violet MacDermot (1978).
- **Bruce Codex** (Bodleian Library MS Bruce 96). Brought from Upper Egypt by James Bruce in 1769. Contains the two *Books of Jeu* and an untitled tractate sometimes called the *Anonymous Treatise*.
- **Berlin Codex** (Papyrus Berolinensis 8502). Purchased in Cairo in 1896, not published until 1955. Contains the *Gospel of Mary*, the *Apocryphon of John* (short recension), the *Sophia of Jesus Christ*, and the *Act of Peter*.

These codices are Coptic translations of earlier Greek originals. They date from the fourth to fifth centuries and preserve material that otherwise would survive only through the polemicists.

## 3. The Nag Hammadi library

In December 1945, near the village of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, a peasant named Muhammad Ali al-Samman uncovered a sealed jar containing thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices. The library comprises fifty-two tractates (forty-six distinct works after duplicates are removed), copied in Coptic in the mid-fourth century. Most are translations from Greek. Notable tractates include the *Gospel of Thomas*, the *Gospel of Philip*, the *Gospel of Truth*, the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Hypostasis of the Archons*, and the *Tripartite Tractate*.

## 4. Surviving and adjacent traditions

- **Mandaean literature**. The *Ginza Rabba*, the *Book of John*, and the *Canonical Prayerbook*; a living liturgical corpus.
- **Manichaean fragments**. Coptic codices from Medinet Madi (1930), Parthian and Middle Persian fragments from Turfan, and the Cologne Mani-Codex.
- **Hermetic writings**. The Greek *Corpus Hermeticum* and the Latin *Asclepius* share vocabulary and cosmology with Gnostic texts without being strictly gnostic.

A careful student learns to triangulate: what Irenaeus says about the Valentinians, what the *Tripartite Tractate* from Nag Hammadi says in their own voice, and what Clement preserves from Theodotus — read together, they yield a far richer picture than any single source.
