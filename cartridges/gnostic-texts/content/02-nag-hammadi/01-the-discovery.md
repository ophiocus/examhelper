# The Nag Hammadi Discovery

## December 1945

Near the cliffs of Jabal al-Ṭarif, about ten kilometers from the Upper Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi, a peasant named **Muhammad Ali al-Samman** was digging for *sabakh* — a nitrate-rich soil used as fertilizer. Beneath a large boulder he struck a sealed earthenware jar nearly a meter tall. Fearing a *jinn*, he hesitated; hoping for gold, he broke it open. What fell out was a bundle of thirteen leather-bound papyrus codices.

In the months that followed, some pages were burned as kindling by his mother. Most were sold piecemeal to antiquities dealers in Cairo. The story of their recovery, authentication, and reassembly — including a blood feud, the arrest of Muhammad Ali for an unrelated killing, and decades of scholarly infighting over publication rights — was not fully pieced together until James M. Robinson's work in the 1970s.

## The codices

The thirteen codices, now housed at the Coptic Museum in Cairo, are designated **NHC I through XIII**. They contain **fifty-two tractates**, of which six are duplicates; the library preserves **forty-six distinct works**. Codex XIII survives only as eight leaves tucked inside the cover of Codex VI.

Paleographic analysis dates the manuscripts to the mid-fourth century (approximately 340–360 CE). The Coptic dialects represented are mostly **Sahidic** and **Subakhmimic**. Almost all the texts are translations from Greek originals that are, in some cases, considerably earlier — the *Gospel of Thomas*, for instance, may go back to the late first or early second century.

## Why were they buried?

The leading hypothesis connects the burial to the **Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter of Athanasius** (367 CE), in which the Patriarch of Alexandria fixed the canon of the New Testament and ordered the destruction of non-canonical books. The codices were found near the ruins of a **Pachomian monastery** (St. Pachomius founded coenobitic monasticism in this region in the 320s), and several cover linings contain Pachomian letters. A plausible reconstruction: monks who valued these texts, unwilling to burn them, sealed them in a jar and hid them in the cliffs.

This reconstruction is contested. Some scholars (notably Nicola Denzey Lewis) argue the jar was a private burial deposit rather than a monastic concealment. Either way, the books lay undisturbed for roughly sixteen hundred years.

## Publication history

- **1945–1955.** Dispersal, private sales, the Jung Codex controversy.
- **1956.** First tractate published: the *Gospel of Truth*.
- **1972–1984.** Facsimile edition of all thirteen codices under UNESCO sponsorship.
- **1977.** *The Nag Hammadi Library in English*, ed. James M. Robinson — first complete English translation, revised in 1988 and again in the *Nag Hammadi Scriptures* (Meyer, 2007).

The library instantly doubled the corpus of primary gnostic material available to scholarship and rendered obsolete nearly every synthesis written before 1950.
