# Key Tractates

Of the fifty-two texts in the library, a smaller set has come to define the scholarly and popular image of Nag Hammadi. What follows is a brief guide to the most important.

## The Gospel of Thomas (NHC II,2)

A collection of **114 sayings** of Jesus, introduced as "the hidden words that the living Jesus spoke and Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down." No passion narrative, no miracles — only *logia*, many paralleled in the synoptics (especially Luke) and many with no canonical parallel.

- Saying 1: "Whoever finds the interpretation of these sayings will not taste death."
- Saying 3: "The kingdom is inside you and outside you."
- Saying 77: "Split a piece of wood, and I am there; lift up the stone, and you will find me there."
- Saying 113: The kingdom "is spread out upon the earth, and people do not see it."

Dates proposed range from c. 60 CE (Crossan, Pagels) to the mid-second century. The Greek fragments (P.Oxy. 1, 654, 655) confirm a pre-200 CE Greek original behind the Coptic translation.

## The Apocryphon of John (NHC II,1; III,1; IV,1; BG 8502,2)

The single most important document for reconstructing **Sethian mythology**. A frame narrative in which the risen Christ appears to John son of Zebedee and reveals the structure of the Pleroma, the fall of Sophia, the birth of Yaldabaoth (the demiurge), and the hidden history of Adam, Seth, and Noah.

Surviving in **four manuscripts** (three Nag Hammadi + Berlin Codex), in two recensions — short and long — the *Apocryphon of John* was clearly already a classic by the fourth century. Irenaeus paraphrases an earlier form of this myth in *Against Heresies* I.29, confirming that a version circulated before 180 CE.

## The Gospel of Philip (NHC II,3)

Not a narrative but an anthology of Valentinian reflections on sacraments, marriage, and the bridal chamber. Famously obscure; made notorious by Saying 59, which describes Mary Magdalene as the companion (*koinōnos*) of the Savior whom he "loved more than all the disciples and kissed on the [lacuna]."

The five sacraments listed in §68 — baptism, chrism, eucharist, redemption, bridal chamber — have no full parallel elsewhere.

## The Gospel of Truth (NHC I,3; XII,2)

A homiletic meditation, attributed by most scholars to **Valentinus himself**. No cosmogony, no system of aeons — rather a rhapsodic treatment of the gospel (*euangelion*) as the dawn of *gnosis* in a world of forgetting. Compared stylistically to the Johannine prologue.

## The Hypostasis of the Archons (NHC II,4)

A Sethian rewriting of Genesis 1–6. The archons, rulers of the material world, attempt to violate Eve; she laughs at them, leaves her reflection behind, and becomes the spiritual Eve. Contains a dialogue between Norea (daughter of Eve) and the angel Eleleth, who reveals the origin of the archons from Sophia's error.

## Thunder, Perfect Mind (NHC VI,2)

A revelation poem spoken in the first person by a female divine voice whose self-descriptions are deliberately paradoxical:

> "For I am the first and the last. / I am the honored one and the scorned one. / I am the whore and the holy one. / I am the wife and the virgin."

The speaker is not named. Proposed identifications include Sophia, Eve, Barbelo, and Isis. Unlike most Nag Hammadi texts, it has no obvious Sethian or Valentinian framework.

## The Tripartite Tractate (NHC I,5)

The longest sustained **Valentinian system** in a gnostic voice — a counterpart to Irenaeus's outsider summary. Divided into three sections on the Father, creation, and the economy of salvation. Notable for softening the Valentinian dualism: the Logos, not Sophia, is the fallen aeon, and his error is treated with more sympathy than in other accounts.

## Zostrianos (NHC VIII,1) and Allogenes (NHC XI,3)

Two Sethian **ascent apocalypses** in which a visionary travels through the aeons and receives names and seals. Porphyry's *Life of Plotinus* (ch. 16) mentions that Plotinus and his circle refuted "Zostrianos" and "Allogenes" as Gnostic forgeries; the Nag Hammadi tractates are almost certainly the texts he names. This places them before Plotinus's teaching career in Rome (244–270 CE).
