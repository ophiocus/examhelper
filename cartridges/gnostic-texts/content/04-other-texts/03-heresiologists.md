# The Heresiologists

Before the manuscript finds of the last three centuries, Gnosticism was almost entirely an artifact of its enemies' writing. The Church Fathers who set out to refute the gnostics preserved them — in distorted form, polemically selected, but sometimes at length and sometimes verbatim.

## Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 130 – c. 202)

Bishop of Lyon, born in Smyrna, a disciple of Polycarp. His major work, traditionally cited as *Adversus Haereses* ("Against Heresies"), properly titled *On the Detection and Overthrow of the Knowledge Falsely So-Called*, was written around **180 CE**.

Book I is the fundamental document.

- **I.1–8.** A detailed systematic summary of **Valentinian** teaching, drawing on a report of the school of Ptolemy. This is the single longest ancient account of the thirty-aeon system and the fall of Sophia.
- **I.11–12.** Variant Valentinian systems.
- **I.13–21.** Marcus the Magician and his followers; their rites and their number-speculations.
- **I.23–24.** Simon Magus, Menander, Saturninus, Basilides.
- **I.25.** Carpocrates.
- **I.26.** Cerinthus, the Ebionites, the Nicolaitans.
- **I.29.** A **Barbelo-gnostic** system extremely close to the *Apocryphon of John*. Since Irenaeus wrote c. 180, this passage demonstrates that a form of the *Apocryphon of John* was in circulation before that date.
- **I.30.** The **Ophite-Sethian** system, with Yaldabaoth, his seven sons, and the serpent of Eden as bringer of knowledge.

Irenaeus knows what he is summarizing. His reports can be checked, where we possess Nag Hammadi parallels, and they check out with remarkable accuracy.

## Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170 – c. 235)

A Roman presbyter, possibly anti-pope; a fierce polemicist with a strong taste for **source-quotation**. His *Refutation of All Heresies* (the *Philosophumena*) was rediscovered in a Greek manuscript on Mount Athos in 1842, with Books II and III still missing.

- Hippolytus's distinctive contribution is his willingness to **quote his opponents at length**. He preserves:
  - A long excerpt from the **Naassene** commentary on a Phrygian hymn to Attis, a dense and poetic text that blends gnostic Christianity with pagan mystery imagery.
  - The **Peratae** system, with elaborate astrological-gnostic cosmology.
  - The **Apophasis Megale** ("Great Declaration") attributed to **Simon Magus**.
  - A detailed Valentinian account that partly overlaps with Irenaeus but preserves independent material.

Modern scholars treat Hippolytus's source-quotations as primary evidence, alongside the Nag Hammadi and Berlin texts.

## Clement of Alexandria (c. 150 – c. 215)

Clement is unique among the Fathers in his **sympathy** for the gnostic project, even as he opposes particular gnostic teachings. His *Stromata* ("Miscellanies") is full of Valentinian and Basilidian citations.

- The **Excerpta ex Theodoto** ("Excerpts from Theodotus") are a notebook of authentic **Valentinian** passages, probably copied verbatim from a follower of Valentinus, with Clement's own marginal comments. This is the only place in antiquity we read Valentinians in their own undiluted voice.
- Clement also preserves the **Letter of Ptolemy to Flora**, a Valentinian treatise on the Mosaic Law that articulates a tripartite division of the Law into divine, Mosaic, and elder traditions.

## Origen (c. 185 – c. 253)

Origen's *Commentary on John* engages, book by book, with the lost Valentinian commentary of **Heracleon** — the earliest known continuous commentary on a New Testament book. We know Heracleon's exegesis only from Origen's quotations and paraphrases, but the preserved material is enough to reconstruct his method in detail.

## Tertullian (c. 155 – c. 240)

North African, Latin, rhetorically brilliant, polemically ruthless. His *Against the Valentinians*, *Against Marcion*, *Against Hermogenes*, and *On the Flesh of Christ* are polemical rather than expository. Tertullian is valuable for dating (*Against Marcion* in its various recensions) and for vivid rhetorical portraits, but less valuable than Irenaeus or Hippolytus for reconstructing systems.

## Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 310 – 403)

Bishop of Salamis on Cyprus. His *Panarion* ("Medicine Chest Against All Heresies") is an encyclopedic catalogue of **eighty heresies**, written around 375. Epiphanius writes late, copies earlier sources, and frequently garbles what he copies. But he also preserves unique material:

- A long quotation from the **Gospel of the Egyptians** (the heresiological work, not the Nag Hammadi one) in *Panarion* 62.
- Extensive material on the **Borborites** and other sects otherwise unknown.
- The *Gospel of Judas* is known to Irenaeus and to Epiphanius before the recent manuscript find; Epiphanius preserves details missing from Irenaeus.

Epiphanius is the late-antique encyclopedist of heresy, and his *Panarion* was the main reference work for the topic down to the nineteenth century.
