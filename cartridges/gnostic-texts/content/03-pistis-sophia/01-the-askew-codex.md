# The Askew Codex

## The manuscript

The *Pistis Sophia* is preserved in a single manuscript: **British Library MS Add. 5114**, known as the **Askew Codex** after its eighteenth-century owner.

- **Acquired** by Dr. Anthony Askew, a physician and manuscript collector, in London around **1772**, reputedly from a bookseller.
- **Purchased** by the British Museum from Askew's estate in 1785.
- **Physical description:** 178 leaves of parchment (not papyrus), Coptic in the Sahidic dialect, late fourth or early fifth century.
- **Language of composition:** Greek. The Coptic we possess is a translation, probably made in the late third or fourth century.

The codex is a near-complete manuscript — only a handful of leaves are missing — which makes it unusual among gnostic survivals. The binding is gone, but the text block is intact.

## Editions and translations

- **M. G. Schwartze and J. H. Petermann** (1851). First edition with Latin translation.
- **Carl Schmidt** (1905, reissued 1925). The standard critical edition, with German translation.
- **G. R. S. Mead** (1896, revised 1921). First English translation; still widely reprinted, though its theosophical framing is dated.
- **Violet MacDermot** (1978, in the *Nag Hammadi Studies* series). The modern English scholarly edition, with Schmidt's Coptic text on facing pages.

Chapter and page numbering in modern scholarship follows the division introduced by Schmidt. Older citations (Mead, pre-1905) may use the Schwartze–Petermann pagination.

## Structure of the work

The *Pistis Sophia* is not a single treatise but a **composite of four books**, bound together in the Askew Codex without title pages or clear transitions.

- **Books I and II** form a continuous dialogue. The risen Christ, eleven years after the resurrection, has descended from the regions of light and now converses with his disciples on the Mount of Olives. The central narrative of these books is the **fall and restoration of Pistis Sophia** — an aeon of the thirteenth aeon who descended after a light-power, was deceived by the lion-faced archon Authades, and cried out thirteen repentances before Christ raised her back.
- **Book III** continues the dialogue but shifts to questions on the mysteries — the rituals, names, and seals by which souls ascend.
- **Book IV** is clearly older and stylistically different. It contains detailed descriptions of the chastisements in the outer darkness and the regions of the archons. Most scholars regard it as an independent, earlier composition bound into the codex.

## Date

Scholarly consensus places the composition of Books I–III in the **late third century** (roughly 250–300 CE) and Book IV somewhat earlier, perhaps mid-third century. This makes the *Pistis Sophia* contemporary with or slightly later than the main Sethian tractates of Nag Hammadi, and it shows the influence of developed Christian theological vocabulary.

## Place in the dialogue

The *Pistis Sophia* belongs to a distinctive subgenre of **post-resurrection revelation dialogues**, in which the risen Christ remains on earth (eleven years, in this case) to deliver teaching too esoteric for his earthly ministry. Parallels include the *Apocryphon of John*, the *Sophia of Jesus Christ*, and the *Gospel of Mary*. The *Pistis Sophia* is by far the longest and most elaborate of the surviving examples.
