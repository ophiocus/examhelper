# Structure of the Four Books

## Books I–II: The Pistis Sophia narrative

The frame: for eleven years after the resurrection, Jesus has taught his disciples only "up to the places of the First Commandment and up to the places of the First Mystery." Now, on the fifteenth day of the month of Tobe, he summons them to the Mount of Olives and announces that he will at last reveal what lies above.

The central story unfolded across these two books is the **fall of Pistis Sophia**. Sophia is an aeon dwelling in the thirteenth aeon — above the twelve aeons of the rulers (*archons*) but below the Treasury of Light. Looking upward, she sees a light-power and is drawn toward it. The lion-faced **Authades** ("the Self-willed"), an archon of the twelve, takes offense at her ambition and hurls a false light-power to deceive her. She descends, is seized by its emanations, and falls into the regions of chaos.

From chaos she utters **thirteen repentances** — long lyrical laments modeled on the Psalms and the Odes of Solomon, each answered by an interpretation from one of the disciples. Christ progressively lifts her up: first to a lower place, then higher, until at last she is restored to her own region.

Structurally, the text moves in a repeating rhythm:

1. **Christ speaks** — a passage of mythological narrative or a hymn spoken by Sophia.
2. **A disciple asks to interpret** — frequently Mary Magdalene, sometimes Peter, John, Philip, Thomas, Matthew, or Martha.
3. **Christ responds** — often with warm praise if the interpretation is correct ("well done, Mary, thou blessed one"; "thou art a pneumatic, Mary, who shall inherit the whole kingdom of light").
4. The cycle repeats.

This structure makes the *Pistis Sophia* as much a catechism of exegesis as a myth: it teaches its readers how to interpret prophetic and hymnic texts gnostically by showing the disciples doing so.

## Book III: The mysteries

Book III shifts register. The narrative of Sophia is essentially complete, and the disciples now question the risen Christ on the **mysteries** — the rites, names, seals, baptisms, and spiritual offices by which a soul can ascend past the archons. The Five Trees, the Five Helpers, the Seven Amens, the Three Amens, the Twin Saviors, the Treasury of Light, the Ineffable, the First Mystery, the First Commandment — an elaborate vocabulary is unfolded, not always with internal consistency.

This is the part of the *Pistis Sophia* that most resembles a practical manual: it catalogues inheritance in the kingdom of light, the fate of souls who have or have not received particular mysteries, and the sins for which repentance is still possible.

## Book IV: The chastisements

Book IV stands somewhat apart. It is stylistically rougher, uses a different vocabulary (notably the term *aerodios*, "aerial", for the rulers of the outer darkness), and treats material — the twelve dungeons of the outer darkness, the amounts of years souls are chastised for specific sins, the geography of the spheres of fate — that Books I–III presuppose but do not describe.

Most scholars regard Book IV as an **older, independent composition** bound into the codex by a later compiler. It shows an earlier stage of the same tradition: the cosmos is already populated with the same kinds of archons and spheres, but the technical vocabulary of "the First Mystery", "the Ineffable", and so on has not yet developed.

## A note on protagonists

Of the disciples, **Mary Magdalene speaks more often than all the others combined**. Forty-six of the seventy-odd disciple-questions in Books I–III are hers. Peter at several points objects — famously in chapter 36: "My Lord, we cannot bear this woman, for she takes the opportunity from us and does not let any of us speak, but she speaks many times." Christ does not rebuke Mary; he continues to call on her.

This prominence of Mary, paired with similar material in the *Gospel of Mary* and the *Gospel of Philip*, has made the *Pistis Sophia* a central text for any reconstruction of Mary Magdalene's place in early Christian memory.
